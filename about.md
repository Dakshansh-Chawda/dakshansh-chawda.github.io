From 'Stephen Hawking is a fictitious character' to using quantum field theory, advanced mathematics, and machine learning to solve challenging problems in theoretical physics—I'm a PhD student at the University of Milan working in theoretical particle physics phenomenology. My research focuses on neural network-based parton distributions and novel theoretical/computational methods in QFT, but I'm broadly interested in fundamental physics and how modern machine learning reshapes our approach to it.

## Background & Journey
{: .tight}

I am a PhD student in theoretical physics at the University of Milano, Italy. My journey into physics began with the strong conviction that 'Stephen Hawking' is a fictitious character when I came across an interview of his in my highschool textbook. I carried that conviction for the next two years until when we got a broadband internet connection at home.<br />
On a perfectly random weekday, I did a perfectly random google search for 'Stephen Hawking' and was surprised to find transcripts of his lectures (spoiler: he was real). I was lucky that at that time we had just covered Euclidean geometry at school, and in one of his lectures he talked about how the sum of internal angles of a triangle can exceed 180 degrees - Oh such an abomination! - an insult to the sweet Euclidean geometry, I said to myself. But the more I tried to find evidence against it the more I realised how beautiful it actually is - soon i was deep into the rabit hole of internet surfing, diving through articles I didn't fully understand, yet discovering that even partial comprehension could spark genuine curiosity and deeper learning.

Fast forward to today, I've developed a strong passion for the intersection of physics, mathematics and computational methods. The realization that modern machine learning can tackle problems previously considered intractable has been transformative, reshaping how I approach physics research. However, I still find the pen and paper calculations more beautiful than the monochromatic screen of vscode.

Currently, I focus on developing and refining neural network-based approaches for extracting parton distribution functions from experimental data — work that combines theoretical physics with cutting-edge machine learning techniques.
